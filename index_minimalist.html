• The Picture of Dorian Gray by Oscar Wilde
    • I don't want to be at the mercy of my emotions. I want to use them, to enjoy them, and to dominate them.

      Included in this edition are contemporary reviews, critiques, and editorials, along with many of Oscar Wilde's personal replies, in a collection originally edited by Stuart Mason (Art and Morality: a Record of the Discussion Which Followed the Publication of Dorian Gray), and published in 1908. Such discourse provides a rich context for the society in which Wilde lived.
  • Lud-In-The-Mist by Hope Mirrlees
    • The story she relates, fantastic and dreamlike, takes place in a land unknown long ago and describes how a nation of solid burghers were in danger from their neighbours the fairies. No pretty sprites these, but agitating creatures who set matter-of-fact citizens dreaming, pervert an academy full of young ladies and tempt worthy hucksters to dally with beauty. Fairy fruit which sends men mad is being smuggled into Dorimare.

      —The Spectator, November 27, 1926

      This edition includes Paris: a Poem, Mirrlees's thoroughly Modernist poem published by Leonard and Virginia Woolf, written before T.S. Eliot's Waste Land.
  • The Vampyre by Dr. John William Polidori
    • 'Remember your oath!'—upon her neck and breast was blood, and upon her throat were the marks of teeth having opened the vein:—to this the men pointed, crying, simultaneously struck with horror, 'A Vampyre! a Vampyre!'

      Conceived on the same stormy night as Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, Polidori was personal doctor to Lord Byron. Indeed, Polidori took great inspiration from a fragment Byron wrote and took the opportunity to expand upon it. This fragment of Byron's is included in this edition. Several vampire tropes find their origins here.
